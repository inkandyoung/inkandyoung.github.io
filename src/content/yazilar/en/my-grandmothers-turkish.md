My nana never studied Turkish. She would have laughed at the idea. But when she wanted me to hurry, she said *hajde*. When she was worried, she had *dert*. When something was beautiful, it was *šeftelija*, like a ripe peach. When she served coffee, she brought it on a *tepsija* with a *fildžan* for each guest and a glass of water nobody was allowed to forget.

I grew up in Edinburgh and spoke to her in a careful, school-learned Bosnian. It was only when I began studying Turkish at university, in a cold seminar room with a radiator that clanked like a tram, that I realised how much of it I already knew.

## Finding her in the dictionary

The first lesson was about the home. *Kapı*, door. *Pencere*, window. *Bahçe*, garden. *Avlu*, courtyard. I wrote each one down and next to it, without thinking, the word my nana used: *kapija*, *pendžer*, *bašča*, *avlija*. My teacher, a patient woman from Konya, leaned over my notebook and smiled. "Ah," she said. "You have a Bosnian grandmother."

It became a game. Every new Turkish word I learned, I checked against her vocabulary. Sometimes there was a match, sometimes a near miss, sometimes a beautiful mistranslation: *merak*, which in Turkish is curiosity or worry, in her mouth meant delight, a passion for something.

## The language between

Linguists call these words *turcizmi*. To me they are something warmer: a third language, made of the overlap between two. It is not quite Turkish and not quite Bosnian. It is the language of a woman who never left the Balkans, spoken by a granddaughter who was never really from there, recognised in a classroom in Scotland by a teacher from central Anatolia.

My nana died the spring I passed my Turkish exam. At the funeral, an old man from her village said to me, *Allah rahmet eylesin*. I answered him in Turkish. He looked at me, startled, and then nodded, as if I had finally said something in her voice.
